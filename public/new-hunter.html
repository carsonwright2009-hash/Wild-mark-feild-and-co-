Your first hunt: a beginner's guide

A plain-language checklist for the weeks before your first season. Rules differ a lot between states and even between hunting units, so use this as a starting list and confirm every rule with your state wildlife agency.

1. Learn the rules and the skills

  • Hunter education. Most states require a hunter education course for new hunters, often with an age cutoff. Many offer an online part followed by an in-person field day. Book early, because classes fill up before the season.
  • Know your species. Learn how to tell your game animal from lookalikes, what is legal to take (sex, size, number), and what the season dates and legal hours are.
  • Practice with your equipment. Shoot at the distances you expect to hunt, from the positions you will actually use. Practice until you can place shots reliably.
  • Find a mentor. An experienced hunter can save you from many beginner mistakes. Many state agencies run mentored hunts for new hunters.

2. Licenses, tags and land

  • Buy your hunting license and any tags or permits from your state wildlife agency. Some species use a draw, with application deadlines months before the season.
  • Confirm where you may hunt: public land boundaries, private land permission, and any special rules for your unit.
  • Check fire restrictions, road closures and any rules on camping, vehicles and weapons types for your area.
  • Carry your license, tags and hunter education card on the hunt.

3. Safety basics

  • Treat every firearm as loaded. Keep the muzzle pointed in a safe direction, keep your finger off the trigger until you are on target and ready to shoot, and be sure of your target and what is beyond it.
  • Wear blaze orange where it is required or advised for your game and weapon. Check your state's rules.
  • Tell someone your plan: where you are parking, your route and when you will be back. Use the check-in plan on the Wildmark kit page.
  • Carry first-aid and light: a first-aid kit with a tourniquet, a headlamp with spare batteries, a way to start a fire and an emergency blanket.
  • Know how to navigate. Phones lose signal and batteries die. Carry a paper map and compass, and download offline maps.
  • If you use a tree stand, wear a full-body harness every time.

4. Gear that matters most

You do not need everything on day one. Start with the things that keep you safe, warm and legal, then add comfort and convenience over time.

  • Legal and safety items: license and tags, blaze orange if needed, hearing protection, first-aid kit, headlamp, fire starter, emergency blanket and whistle.
  • Clothing in layers that match the weather: a base layer, an insulating layer and a rain shell. Avoid cotton.
  • Boots that are broken in. Blisters end hunts.
  • Water, food and a way to purify more water on longer trips.
  • Knife, game bags, gloves and a way to carry meat out if you take an animal.

6. On the day

  • Get to your spot before first light, and move slowly and quietly.
  • Take only a shot that you are sure you can make cleanly, and only at a legal animal that you have positively identified.
  • If you take an animal, field-dress it promptly, keep the meat cool, clean and dry, and follow your state's rules on tagging and on proof of sex and species.
  • Check in with your contact when you are back, even if you took nothing. A good day is one where everyone gets home.
